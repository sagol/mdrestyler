## Narration

- Point of view and tense: first person, past tense, a boy telling his own story to the reader. The present tense comes in for general remarks, for habits, and for the speech tag.
- Distance and interiority: close and outward. He tells what he did, saw and heard, in order. He seldom examines himself at length, and when he reasons about right and wrong he does it in practical, homely terms. Inference: the reader often understands more than the narrator does, and the humor depends on that gap.
- How emotion is handled (named, embodied, deflected): named in plain words (lonesome, glad, scared, comfortable) or shown through the body; often turned straight into the next practical step.
- How thought moves within a paragraph; hesitation, self-correction and mixed feeling: by addition. Clauses follow one another joined by and, then and semicolons, in the order things happened or came to mind. He corrects himself in passing with a dash and carries on. A general rule tends to come from a homely comparison.

## Rhythm

- Sentence movement, and how it shifts under pressure or in reflection: long chains of short clauses set against short flat sentences, so sentence length swings widely from one sentence to the next. Quiet passages run as one long catalogue of what he saw and heard; action comes as a run of quick verbs.
- Paragraphing by scene type: narration paragraphs of a few sentences, one beat of the scene each; dialogue in short paragraphs, with the tag before or after the speech.
- Variation between quiet scenes, argument, intimacy and crisis: quiet and lyrical moments keep the plain vocabulary and are usually closed by a practical or comic remark; argument is carried by the dialogue.

## Diction

- Register and vocabulary band: vernacular and spoken, short words, a narrow vocabulary that repeats rather than varies. Grammar is nonstandard and consistent: double negatives; was after plural subjects; present-tense forms used as past; participles with an a- prefix; adjectives used as adverbs.
- Words and constructions the voice favors (described; quote only own books): regional words for thinking and judging (reckon, allow, judge); words for distance and time from rural speech (yonder, by-and-by); intensifiers taken from ordinary talk rather than from -ly adverbs, which are rare; sentences that open on and, then, so or but; vague quantity (kind of, pretty, most for almost).

## Imagery

- Where the images come from; how often figurative language appears: the river, weather, woods, animals, food and household work. Mostly concrete sensory detail, especially sounds and smells, listed as they come. Similes are infrequent and homely; comic exaggeration does the work of metaphor.

## Dialogue

- How speech is set out and tagged: double quotation marks; the tag is nearly always says, in the present tense, and sometimes inverted.
- How characters are told apart: by dialect and grammar, by what they want, and by how long they talk.
- How a reply answers, evades, interrupts or changes a relationship: replies are direct and literal, and the comedy comes from that literalness; speech can run to a long single paragraph.

## Structure

- Chapter openings and endings; scene breaks; summary against scene: chapters often open by summarizing the time that passed, then narrow to one event; they end on a turn, a line of speech or a dry closing remark.

## Tone and humor

- Deadpan. The narrator is serious about things the reader finds funny, and plain about things the reader finds moving. Satire of adults and institutions comes through his uncomprehending report.

## Never

- Author-stated exclusions: none; this profile was built from a reference book, with no author direction. Rare in the source rather than excluded: -ly adverbs, abstract and Latinate vocabulary, extended analysis of feeling.

## Evidence and limits
