"Get up! What are you about?"

I opened my eyes and looked about me, trying, with such wits as I could gather, to make out where I was. It was after sunrise, and I had been very soundly asleep. Pap was standing over me, looking sour, and looking, as I could not fail to observe, sick as well. He said:

"What are you doing with this gun?"

I judged that he knew nothing whatever of what he had himself been doing, and so I answered:

"Somebody tried to get in, so I was lying in wait for him."

"Why didn't you rouse me?"

"Well, I tried to—but I couldn't; I couldn't so much as stir you."

"Well—all right. Don't stand there jawing all day. Out with you, and see if there's a fish on the lines for breakfast. I'll be along in a minute."

He unlocked the door, and I took myself off up the river-bank. I noticed, as I went, certain pieces of limbs and such things floating down, together with a sprinkling of bark; and from these I knew, beyond any doubt, that the river had begun to rise. I reckoned, as I remember, that I should have great times of it now if only I had been over at the town. The June rise had always, in those days, been luck for me; for as soon as it began there would come cordwood floating down, and pieces of log rafts—sometimes as many as a dozen logs together—so that all one had to do was to catch them and sell them to the wood-yards and the sawmill.

I went on along the bank with one eye out for pap and the other for whatever the rise might fetch along. Then, quite suddenly, there came a canoe—a beauty, too, some thirteen or fourteen foot long, riding high, like a duck. I shot head-first off the bank, like a frog, with all my clothes on, and struck out for her. I fully expected, I confess, that there would be somebody lying down in it, for people often did that by way of fooling folks, and when a chap had pulled a skiff out nearly to it they would rise up and laugh at him. It was not so this time. It was, sure enough, a drift-canoe, and I climbed in and paddled her ashore. The old man, I thought to myself, would be glad when he saw this—she was worth ten dollars. When I reached the shore, however, pap was not yet in sight; and as I was running her into a little creek like a gully, all hung over with vines and willows, another idea struck me. I judged that I would hide her well, and then, instead of taking to the woods when I ran off, I would go down the river some fifty mile and camp in one place for good, and so spare myself the rough time of tramping on foot.

The spot was pretty near the shanty, and I fancied the whole while that I heard the old man coming; I got her hidden, notwithstanding, and then I stepped out and looked round a bunch of willows—and there was the old man, a little way down the path, in the very act of drawing a bead on a bird with his gun. He had, therefore, seen nothing.

When he came up I was hard at work taking up a "trot" line. He abused me a little for my slowness; I told him, however, that I had fallen into the river, and that this was what had kept me so long, for I knew perfectly well that he would see I was wet, and that he would then be asking questions. We got five catfish off the lines and went home.

While we lay down after breakfast to make up our sleep, both of us being pretty thoroughly worn out, I fell to thinking that if I could contrive some means of keeping pap and the widow from trying to follow me, it would be a surer thing than trusting to luck to get far enough off before they missed me; all kinds of things, you see, might happen. For a while I saw no way at all; by-and-by, however, pap raised himself up for a minute to drink another barrel of water, and he said:

"Another time a man comes prowling round here, you rouse me, you hear? That man wasn't here for any good. I'd have shot him. Next time you rouse me—you hear?"

Then he dropped down and went to sleep again; but what he had been saying gave me precisely the idea I wanted. I said to myself: I can fix it now so that nobody will think of following me.

About twelve o'clock we turned out and went along up the bank. The river was coming up pretty fast, with lots of driftwood going by on the rise, and by-and-by there came along part of a log raft—nine logs fast together. We went out with the skiff and towed it ashore, and then we had dinner. Anybody but pap would have waited and seen the day through, so as to catch more stuff; that, however, was not pap's style. Nine logs were enough for one time; he must shove right over to town and sell. So he locked me in, took the skiff, and started off towing the raft at about half-past three. I judged that he would not come back that night. I waited until I reckoned he had got a good start; then out came my saw, and I went to work on that log again. Before he was on the other side of the river I was out of the hole, and he and his raft were by then no more than a speck on the water, far off yonder.

I took the sack of corn meal to the place where the canoe was hidden, shoved the vines and branches apart, and put it in; then I did the same with the side of bacon, and then with the whisky-jug. I took all the coffee and sugar there was, and all the ammunition; I took the wadding; I took the bucket and the gourd; I took a dipper and a tin cup, my old saw and two blankets, the skillet and the coffee-pot. I took fish-lines and matches and other things—everything, in short, that was worth a cent. I cleaned the place out. I wanted an axe, but there was none, only the one out at the woodpile, and I knew very well why I was going to leave that. I fetched out the gun, and with that I was done.

I had worn the ground a good deal in crawling out of the hole and dragging out so many things, and I made that good as well as I could from the outside by scattering dust over the place, which covered up both the smoothness and the sawdust. Then I fitted the piece of log back into its place and put two rocks under it and one against it to hold it there, for it was bent up at that point and did not quite touch the ground. If one stood four or five foot away and did not know it was sawed, one would never notice it; and besides, this was the back of the cabin, where it was hardly likely that anybody would go fooling about.

It was all grass clear to the canoe, so that I had left no track; I followed round to make sure of it. I stood on the bank and looked out over the river. All was safe. So I took the gun and went a little way up into the woods, and I was hunting about for some birds when I saw a wild pig—hogs soon went wild in those bottoms once they had got away from the prairie farms. I shot this fellow and took him into camp.

I took the axe and smashed in the door, beating and hacking it considerably in the doing. I fetched the pig in, took him back nearly to the table, hacked into his throat with the axe, and laid him down on the ground to bleed—I say ground because it was ground, hard packed, with no boards. Next I took an old sack and put a lot of big rocks into it, as many as I could drag, and, starting it from the pig, I dragged it to the door and through the woods down to the river, and dumped it in; and down it sank, out of sight. Anyone could easily see that something had been dragged over the ground. I did wish, I remember, that Tom Sawyer had been there; I knew he would take an interest in this kind of business and throw in the fancy touches. Nobody, in an affair of that sort, could make such a splendid show of himself as Tom Sawyer.
