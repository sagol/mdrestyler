“Get up! What are you about?”

I opened my eyes and looked round me, trying, in the first confusion of waking, to make out where I was. It was already after sun-up, and I had been, I can only suppose, very soundly asleep. Pap was standing over me, and he looked, as I recall him at that moment, both sour and sick. He said:

“What are you doing with this gun?”

It was my judgment, formed on the instant, that he knew nothing whatever of what he had himself been doing; so I answered:

“Somebody tried to get in, so I was lying in wait for him.”

“Why didn’t you rouse me out?”

“Well, I tried to—but I couldn’t; I couldn’t so much as budge you.”

“Well, all right. Don’t stand there talking all day—out with you, and see whether there’s a fish on the lines for breakfast. I’ll be along in a minute.”

He unlocked the door, and I took myself off, with no loss of time, up the river-bank. I noticed, as I went, certain pieces of limbs and such things floating down, and with them a sprinkling of bark; so that I knew, without having to be told, that the river had begun to rise. I reckoned, too, that I should have had great times of it now had I only been over at the town. The June rise had always, in my experience, meant luck for me; for as soon as that rise began there came the cordwood floating down, and pieces of log rafts—sometimes as many as a dozen logs together; so that all one had to do was to catch them and sell them to the wood-yards and the sawmill.

I went along up the bank, accordingly, with one eye out for pap and the other for whatever the rise might fetch along. Then, quite all at once, there came a canoe—a real beauty, too, about thirteen or fourteen foot long, and riding high, as I distinctly remember, like a duck. I shot head-first off the bank like a frog, clothes and all on, and struck out for her. I fully expected, I confess, that there would be somebody lying down in it, since people often did that to fool folks, and when some chap had pulled a skiff out most of the way to it they would raise themselves up and laugh at him. It was not so, however, this time. It was a drift-canoe sure enough, and I climbed in and paddled her ashore. The old man, I thought, would be glad when he saw this—she was worth ten dollars. But when I got to shore pap was not yet in sight; and as I was running her into a little creek, a sort of gully, all hung over with vines and willows, another idea struck me. I judged that I would hide her well, and then, instead of taking to the woods when I ran off, I would go down the river about fifty mile and camp in one place for good, and so spare myself the rough time of tramping on foot.

The place was uncomfortably close to the shanty, and I thought, the whole while, that I heard the old man coming; but I got her hidden. Then I went out and looked round a bunch of willows, and there, a piece down the path, was the old man, just drawing a bead on a bird with his gun. He had not, then, seen anything.

When he came along I was hard at it, taking up a “trot” line. He abused me a little for being so slow; but I told him I had fallen in the river, and that this was what had made me so long. I knew perfectly well that he would see I was wet, and that he would then be asking questions. We got five catfish off the lines and went home.

While we lay off after breakfast to sleep, both of us being very nearly worn out, I fell to thinking that if I could only contrive some way to keep pap and the widow from trying to follow me, it would be a surer thing than trusting to luck to get far enough off before they missed me; for all kinds of things, you see, might happen. For a while, I admit, I saw no way at all; but by-and-by pap raised himself up for a minute to drink another barrel of water, and he said:

“Another time a man comes prowling round here, you rouse me out, do you hear? That man was here for no good. I’d have shot him. Next time you rouse me out—do you hear?”

Then he dropped down and went to sleep again; but what he had been saying gave me—and I felt it at once—the very idea I wanted. I said to myself that I could fix it now so that nobody would think of following me.

About twelve o’clock we turned out and went along up the bank. The river was coming up pretty fast, and there was a great deal of driftwood going by on the rise. By-and-by there came along part of a log raft—nine logs fast together. We went out with the skiff and towed it ashore. Then we had dinner. Anybody but pap would have waited and seen the day through, so as to catch more stuff; but that, decidedly, was not pap’s style. Nine logs were enough for one time; he must shove right over to town and sell. So he locked me in, took the skiff, and started off, towing the raft, at about half-past three. I judged that he would not come back that night. I waited until I reckoned he had got a good start; then I brought out my saw and went to work on that log again. Before he was on the other side of the river I was out of the hole; he and his raft were by then just a speck on the water, away off yonder.

I took the sack of corn meal to where the canoe was hidden, shoved the vines and branches apart, and put it in; then I did the same with the side of bacon, and then with the whisky-jug. I took all the coffee and sugar there was, and all the ammunition; I took the wadding; I took the bucket and the gourd; I took a dipper and a tin cup, and my old saw and two blankets, and the skillet and the coffee-pot. I took fish-lines and matches and other things—everything, in short, that was worth a cent. I cleaned out the place. I wanted an axe, but there was none, only the one out at the woodpile, and I knew quite well why I was going to leave that. I fetched out the gun, and with that I was done.

I had worn the ground a good deal in crawling out of the hole and dragging out so many things. I made that good, therefore, as well as I could from the outside, by scattering dust over the place, which covered up both the smoothness and the sawdust. Then I fixed the piece of log back into its place, and put two rocks under it and one against it to hold it there, for it was bent up at that point and did not quite touch the ground. If you had stood four or five foot away and had not known it was sawed, you would never have noticed it; and besides, this was the back of the cabin, and it was hardly likely that anybody would go fooling around there.

It was all grass clear to the canoe, so that I had left no track. I followed around to make sure of this. I stood on the bank and looked out over the river. All was safe. So I took the gun and went up a piece into the woods, and was hunting about for some birds when I saw a wild pig; hogs, I should explain, soon went wild in those bottoms once they had got away from the prairie farms. I shot this fellow and took him into camp.

I took the axe and smashed in the door, beating it and hacking it considerably in the doing. I fetched the pig in, took him back nearly to the table, hacked into his throat with the axe, and laid him down on the ground to bleed; I say ground because it was ground—hard packed, and without boards. Next I took an old sack and put a lot of big rocks into it—all I could drag—and, starting it from the pig, I dragged it to the door and through the woods down to the river, and dumped it in, and down it sank, out of sight. You could easily see that something had been dragged over the ground. I did wish, and wished it intensely, that Tom Sawyer had been there; I knew he would take an interest in this kind of business, and throw in the fancy touches. Nobody could spread himself like Tom Sawyer in such a thing as that.
