"Get up! What are you about?"

I opened my eyes and looked round, trying, in that first dull confusion of waking, to make out where I was. It was already after sun-up, and I had been, as I distinctly recall, sound asleep; and there was pap, standing over me, looking sour, and looking too, what struck me hardly less, sick. He said:

"What are you doing with this gun?"

I judged that he had no notion at all of what he himself had been doing, and so I said:

"Somebody tried to get in, so I was lying in wait for him."

"Why didn't you rouse me out?"

"Well, I tried to, but I couldn't. I couldn't budge you."

"Well, all right. Don't stand there jawing all day. Out with you, and see if there's a fish on the lines for breakfast. I'll be along in a minute."

He unlocked the door, and I took myself off up the river-bank. I noticed, as I went, some pieces of limbs and other such things floating down, with a sprinkling of bark among them; and I knew by these modest signs that the river had begun to rise. I reckoned, and I recall the reckoning with a certain wistfulness even now, that I should have had great times of it now, had I only been over at the town. For the June rise had always, in those days, been a positive piece of luck for me: no sooner did it begin than down came the cordwood, floating, and pieces of log rafts, sometimes as many as a dozen logs together, so that all one had to do was to catch them and sell them to the wood-yards and the sawmill.

I went on along the bank with one eye out for pap and the other for whatever the rise might bring; and then, quite all at once, there came a canoe, a beauty, too, about thirteen or fourteen feet long, riding high on the water like a duck. I shot head-first off the bank like a frog, clothes and all, and struck out for her. I fully expected, I confess, to find somebody lying down in her, for people often did that by way of fooling folks, and when some unlucky fellow had pulled a skiff nearly out to it they would rise up and laugh at him. But it was not so this time. She was a drift-canoe, sure enough; and I climbed in and paddled her ashore. The old man, I thought, would be glad when he saw her; she was worth ten dollars. But when I reached the shore pap was not yet in sight, and as I was running her into a little creek, a kind of gully all hung over with vines and willows, another idea struck me. I judged that I would hide her well, and then, instead of taking to the woods when I ran off, I would go down the river about fifty miles and camp in one place for good, and so be spared the rough time of tramping on foot.

The spot was pretty close to the shanty, and I thought I heard the old man coming the whole time; but I got her hidden, and then I slipped out and looked round a bunch of willows, and there, a little way down the path, was the old man, just drawing a bead on a bird with his gun. So he had seen nothing.

When he came along I was hard at work taking up a "trot" line. He abused me a little for being so slow; but I told him I had fallen into the river, and that this was what had kept me so long. I knew he would see that I was wet, and that he would then be asking questions. We took five catfish off the lines and went home.

While we lay off after breakfast to sleep, both of us being pretty thoroughly worn out, I fell to thinking that if I could only contrive some means of keeping pap and the widow from trying to follow me, it would be a surer thing than trusting to luck to get far enough off before they missed me; for all kinds of things, as one may readily see, might happen. For a while I saw no way at all; but by-and-by pap raised himself up a minute to drink another barrel of water, and he said:

"Another time a man comes prowling round here, you rouse me out, you hear? That man was here for no good. I'd have shot him. Next time, you rouse me out, you hear?"

Then he dropped down and went to sleep again; but what he had been saying gave me, with a completeness I could scarcely have hoped for, the very idea I wanted. I said to myself: I can fix it now so that nobody will think of following me.

About twelve o'clock we turned out and went along up the bank. The river was coming up pretty fast, with a great deal of driftwood going by on the rise; and by-and-by along came part of a log raft, nine logs fast together. We went out with the skiff and towed it ashore, and then we had dinner. Anybody but pap would have waited and seen the day through, so as to catch more stuff; but that, I need hardly say, was not pap's style. Nine logs were enough for one time; he must shove right over to town and sell. So he locked me in, took the skiff, and started off, towing the raft, at about half-past three. I judged he would not come back that night. I waited until I reckoned he had got a good start; then out came my saw, and I went to work on that log again. Before he was on the other side of the river I was out of the hole, and he and his raft were by then no more than a speck on the water, away off yonder.

I took the sack of corn meal to where the canoe was hidden, shoved the vines and branches apart, and put it in; then I did the same with the side of bacon; then with the whisky-jug. I took all the coffee and sugar there was, and all the ammunition; I took the wadding; I took the bucket and the gourd; I took a dipper and a tin cup, and my old saw and two blankets, and the skillet and the coffee-pot. I took fish-lines and matches and other things, everything, in short, that was worth a cent. I cleaned the place out. I wanted an axe, but there was none, save only the one out at the woodpile, and I knew perfectly well why I was going to leave that one. I fetched out the gun, and with that I was done.

I had worn the ground a good deal in crawling out of the hole and dragging out so many things; so I mended that as well as I could from the outside by scattering dust over the place, which covered up both the smoothness and the sawdust. Then I fixed the piece of log back into its place, and put two rocks under it and one against it to hold it there, for it was bent up at that point and did not quite touch the ground. If you had stood four or five feet away and had not known it was sawed, you would never have noticed it; and besides, this was the back of the cabin, and it was not in the least likely that anybody would go fooling around there.

It was all grass clear to the canoe, so that I had left no track. I followed round to see. I stood on the bank and looked out over the river. All was safe. So I took the gun and went a little way up into the woods, and was hunting about for some birds when I saw a wild pig, for hogs soon went wild in those bottoms once they had got away from the prairie farms. I shot this fellow and took him into camp.

I took the axe and smashed in the door, beating and hacking it considerably in the doing. I fetched the pig in, took him back nearly to the table, hacked into his throat with the axe, and laid him down on the ground to bleed; I say ground because it was ground, hard packed, with no boards. Next I took an old sack and put a lot of big rocks into it, as many as I could drag, and I started it from the pig and dragged it to the door, and through the woods down to the river, and dumped it in; and down it sank, out of sight. Anyone could easily see that something had been dragged over the ground. I did wish, I remember, that Tom Sawyer had been there; I knew he would take an interest in this kind of business, and throw in the fancy touches. Nobody could spread himself as Tom Sawyer could in such an affair as that.
