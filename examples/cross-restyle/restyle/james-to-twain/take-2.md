In them first weeks the days was long. Often, on the best of them, they gave me what I used to call my own hour. That was the hour when my pupils' teatime and bedtime had come and gone, and I had a little stretch to myself before I turned in for good. I liked the folks I was with well enough, but that hour was the thing in the day I liked most; and I liked it best of all when the light was going, or I ought to say when the day hung on and the last birds give their last calls out of the old trees, with the sky all flushed red, and I could take a turn out into the grounds. Then I would enjoy how handsome and grand the place was, and I enjoyed it most like it belonged to me; that amused me, and it flattered me too. It was pleasant at them times to feel easy in my mind and in the right. I reckon, maybe, I liked to think too that by being careful and sensible and proper all round I was giving pleasure to the person whose pressing I had answered, if he ever thought of it at all! What I was doing was just what he had hoped for hard and had asked of me straight out; and finding I could do it after all was a bigger joy than I had looked for. I judge I fancied I was a pretty remarkable young woman, in short, and took comfort in believing it would show plainer to folks by-and-by. Well, I needed to be remarkable, to stand up to the remarkable things that give their first sign before long.

It come square in the middle of my own hour, one afternoon. The children was tucked away and I had come out for my walk. I don't mind owning now that one of the notions that used to go along with me on them rambles was that it would be as charming as a charming story to meet somebody all of a sudden. Somebody would show up there at the turn of a path, and stand in front of me, and smile, and approve. I didn't ask no more than that; I only asked that he should know. And the only way to be sure he knowed would be to see it in his handsome face, and the kind light of it. That was right there before me, the face was, I mean, the first time it happened, at the end of a long June day, when I come out of one of the plantations and in sight of the house and stopped short. What stopped me on the spot, and give me a heap bigger shock than any of my imaginings had allowed for, was the feeling that what I had imagined had turned real in a flash. He did stand there! Only he was high up, away beyond the lawn, at the very top of the tower that little Flora had led me to on that first morning.

That tower was one of a pair. They was square, crenelated things that didn't fit the house, and for some reason folks called one the new and the other the old, though I couldn't see much difference. They stood at opposite ends of the house. I reckon they was probably foolishness, as building goes; but they was saved some by not standing clean apart from the house, and by not being too high and stuck-up. They dated, with their gingerbread kind of oldness, from a romantic revival that was already a respectable ways back. I admired them and had fancies about them, for we could all get some good out of the grandeur of their real battlements, especially when they loomed up through the dusk; but it warn't at no such height as that that the figure I had called up so often seemed to belong best.

It give me two separate gasps, that figure did, there in the clear twilight; I remember it. They come sharp: the shock of my first surprise and the shock of my second. My second was a hard, violent seeing that my first had been a mistake. The man that met my eyes warn't the person I had jumped to think he was. So there come over me such a bewilderment of seeing that, after these years, I can't hope to give you any living view of it. A strange man in a lonesome place is a thing a young woman brought up private is allowed to be afraid of. And the figure that faced me was—a few more seconds made me sure of it—no more anybody else I knowed than it was the picture I had carried in my head. I hadn't seen it in Harley Street. I hadn't seen it anywheres. And the place, in the strangest way in the world, had turned into a solitude on the instant, just by his showing up in it. To me, anyway, setting this down here more deliberate than I ever set it down before, the whole feeling of that minute comes back. It was like everything else in the scene had been struck dead while I took in what I did take in. I can hear it again as I write, the big hush the evening sounds dropped away in. The rooks quit cawing in the gold sky, and the friendly hour lost all its voice for the minute. But there warn't no other change in nature, unless it was a change I seen with a stranger sharpness than common. The gold was still in the sky and the clearness in the air, and the man that looked at me over the battlements was as plain as a picture in a frame. That's how come I thought, quick as anything, of every person he might have been and that he warn't. We stood facing each other across that distance plenty long enough for me to ask myself hard who he was, then; and because I couldn't say, I got to wondering, and in a few instants more the wonder was powerful strong.

The big question afterwards, or one of them, with some kinds of things, is how long they lasted; I know that. Well, this thing of mine, think what you like of it, lasted while I grabbed at a dozen possibilities, and not a one of them made it any better, that I could see, that there had been a person in the house that I didn't know nothing about. And how long had he been there? That was the main thing. It lasted while I just bristled a little, feeling my place required there shouldn't be no such not-knowing and no such person. It lasted while this visitor, anyway—and there was a touch of a strange freedom about him, I remember, and a sign of being at home, in his wearing no hat—seemed to fix me from where he was, with just the question, just the hard looking-over through the fading light, that his own being there stirred up. We was too far apart to call to each other. But there was a minute when, closer up, some challenge between us, breaking the hush, would have been the right thing to come of us staring so straight at each other. He was in one of the corners, the one away from the house, standing very straight, it struck me, with both hands on the ledge. So I seen him, as plain as I see the letters I am making on this page. Then, after a minute, just as if to add to the show, he changed his place, slow. He passed over to the opposite corner of the platform, looking at me hard all the time. Yes, I had the sharpest kind of feeling that he never took his eyes off me the whole way across, and I can see this minute how his hand went from one of the crenelations to the next as he went. He stopped at the other corner, but not so long; and even while he was turning away he was still fixing me, plain. He turned away. That was all I knew.
