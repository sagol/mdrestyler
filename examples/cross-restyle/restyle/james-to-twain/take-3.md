In the first weeks the days were long. On the best of them I used to get what I called my own hour. That was the hour after the children had had their tea and gone to bed, and before I turned in myself for good, when I had a little while alone. I liked the people I was with well enough, but I liked that hour the best of anything in the day; and I liked it best of all when the light was going—or I ought to say, when the day hung on, and the last birds made their last calls out of the old trees into a flushed sky—and I could take a turn in the grounds and enjoy how handsome and stately the place was, almost as if it was mine, which amused me and flattered me too.

It was a pleasure, those times, to feel quiet and in the right. I reckon, too, maybe, it was a pleasure to think that by keeping my own counsel and using good sense and behaving proper all round I was giving pleasure—if he ever thought of it!—to the man whose urging I had given in to. What I was doing was what he had hoped for, hard, and asked me for straight out; and finding I could do it after all turned out a bigger joy than I had looked for. I allow I took myself, in short, for a remarkable young woman, and took comfort in believing it would show some day where more folks could see it. Well, I needed to be remarkable, to stand up to the remarkable things that presently gave their first sign.

It came plump in the middle of my hour, one afternoon. The children were tucked away and I had come out for my walk. I don't mind a bit owning now that one of the notions I used to carry around with me on these rambles was that it would be as charming as a charming story to run up on somebody all of a sudden. Somebody would show up there at the turn of a path and stand in front of me and smile and approve. I didn't ask any more than that. I only asked that he should know; and the only way to be sure he knew would be to see it in his handsome face, and the kind light of it there.

That was the very thing I had in my mind—I mean the face was—when, the first of these times, at the end of a long June day, I came out of one of the plantations and in sight of the house, and stopped short. What stopped me right there, with a shock a good deal bigger than any of my imaginings had made room for, was the feeling that what I had imagined had gone and turned real, quick as a flash. He did stand there! But he was away up high, beyond the lawn, at the very top of the tower little Flora took me to that first morning.

This tower was one of a pair. They were square, crenelated things that didn't fit with anything, and for some reason they called one the new and the other the old, though I couldn't see much difference. They stood at opposite ends of the house, and I judge they was likely absurd, as building goes; only they was saved some by not standing clean off by themselves, and by not being too high and stuck-up. They dated, gingerbread antiques that they were, from a romantic revival that was already a respectable piece of the past. I admired them and had fancies about them, for we could all get some good out of the grandeur of their real battlements, especially when they loomed up through the dusk. All the same, it wasn't up at such a height that the figure I had called up so often seemed to belong best.

It gave me, this figure, in the clear twilight, two separate gasps, I remember, and sharp ones: the shock of my first surprise and then the shock of my second. My second was a violent seeing that my first was a mistake. The man that met my eyes wasn't the one I had jumped to suppose. So there came over me a mixed-up kind of seeing that I can't hope, after all these years, to give any living picture of.

An unknown man in a lonesome place is a thing a young woman brought up private is allowed to be scared of; and the figure facing me was—a few more seconds made me sure of it—no more anybody else I knew than it was the one I had been carrying in my head. I hadn't seen it in Harley Street. I hadn't seen it anywhere.

And the place, in the strangest way in the world, had turned into a solitude that same instant, just by his showing up in it. To me, anyway—and I am telling it here more deliberate than I ever told it before—the whole feel of that minute comes back. It was as if, while I was taking in what I did take in, all the rest of the scene had been struck dead. I can hear again, as I write, how still it got, and the evening sounds dropping away into it. The rooks quit cawing in the gold sky, and the friendly hour lost all its voice, for the minute. But there wasn't any other change in nature, unless it was that I saw things stranger and sharper. The gold was still in the sky and the clearness in the air, and the man looking at me over the battlements was as plain as a picture in a frame. That's how I went over, uncommon quick, every person he might have been and wasn't. We stood facing each other across that distance plenty long enough for me to ask myself hard who he was, then; and, because I couldn't say, to feel a wondering that got powerful strong in a few moments more.

The big question, or one of them, afterward, I know, about some kinds of things, is how long they lasted. Well, this thing of mine, think what you like of it, lasted while I grabbed at a dozen ways it could be, and not one of them made it look any better, that I could see, that there had been in the house—and, most of all, for how long?—somebody I didn't know about. It lasted while I just got my back up a little, feeling that my place required there shouldn't be any such not-knowing and any such person. It lasted while this visitor—and there was a touch of strange freedom, I remember, a sign of being at home, in his not having any hat on—seemed to fix me, from up there, with just the same question, just the same looking-over through the fading light, that his own being there raised.

We were too far apart to call out to each other; but there was a moment when, if we had been nearer, some kind of challenge between us, breaking that hush, would have been the right thing to come of our staring so straight at each other. He was in one of the corners, the one away from the house, standing very straight, as it struck me, with both hands on the ledge. I saw him the way I see the letters I make on this page. Then, after a minute, just as if to add to the show, he slowly changed his place. He went across to the opposite corner of the platform, looking hard at me all the while. Yes, I had the sharpest feeling that the whole way across he never took his eyes off me, and I can see this minute how his hand, as he went, passed from one of the crenelations to the next. He stopped at the other corner, but not so long; and even while he turned away he still kept me fixed, plain. He turned away; that was all I knew.
