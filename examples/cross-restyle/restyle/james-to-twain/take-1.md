In the first weeks the days was long, and the best of them gave me what I used to call my own hour. That was the hour after my pupils had had their tea and gone to bed, and before I turned in for good myself; it was a little stretch of time I had all alone. I liked the people I was with well enough, but I liked that hour better than anything else in the day.

And I liked it best of all when the light was going—or, to put it right, when the day hung on, and the sky was all flushed, and the last birds was making their last calls out of the old trees—and I could take a turn out in the grounds and look at the place. It was a handsome place, and it had a dignity to it, and I enjoyed it most like I owned it; that tickled me, and it flattered me some, too.

It was a pleasure, them times, to feel quiet in myself and to feel I was in the right. No doubt I was thinking too, maybe, how my being careful, and sensible, and proper all round, was giving pleasure to the man that had pressed me, and that I had give in to—if he ever thought about it at all! What I was doing was just what he had set his heart on and asked me for straight out; and when it turned out I could do it, after all, that was a bigger joy than I had looked for. I reckon I took myself for a pretty remarkable young woman, to cut it short, and I was comforted believing that folks would come to see it, more open and public, by-and-by.

Well, I needed to be remarkable, to stand up to the remarkable things that give their first sign pretty soon after.

It come plump in the middle of my own hour, one afternoon. The children was tucked away, and I had come out for my walk.

There was a notion that used to keep me company on them walks, and I don't mind owning to it now, not the least bit. The notion was that it would be as nice as a nice story if I was to meet somebody, all of a sudden. Somebody would come round the turn of a path and stand there in front of me and smile, and think well of me. I didn't ask no more than that. I only asked that he should know; and the only way to be sure he knew was to see it in his handsome face, and the kind light of it there.

I had that right in front of my mind—the face, I mean—that first time, at the end of a long June day, when I come out of one of the plantations to where the house shows, and stopped short. What stopped me in my tracks, and give me a bigger jolt than any of my fancies had ever allowed for, was the feeling that what I had imagined had turned real, quick as a flash. He did stand there!—only he was high up, away beyond the lawn, at the very top of the tower that little Flora had took me to that first morning.

That tower was one of a pair. They was square, with notched battlements along the top, and they didn't fit the rest of the house; and for some reason folks called one the new and the other the old, though I couldn't see much difference. They stood at the two ends of the house, and I reckon they was foolishness, as building goes. But it saved them some that they warn't standing clean off by themselves, and warn't built so high as to be putting on airs. They had a gingerbread kind of oldness, and they come from a time when folks had took up the romantic old styles again; and that time was a good ways back already, far enough to be respectable.

I admired them, and had fancies about them, for we could all get some good out of the grandness of them battlements, which was real ones, especially when they loomed up through the dusk. All the same, it warn't up at that height that the figure I had called up so often seemed to belong best.

This figure, there in the clear twilight, give me two separate gasps, I remember; it was the jolt of my first surprise, and then the jolt of my second. The second was seeing, hard and sudden, that the first was a mistake. The man looking at me warn't the one I had jumped to think he was.

It mixed me all up, what I was seeing, and after all these years I can't hope to give you any live picture of it. A young woman that's been raised private is let off for being scared of a strange man in a lonesome place; and this one—a few more seconds made me sure of it—warn't anybody else I knew, no more than he was the face I'd had in my mind. I hadn't seen him in Harley Street. I hadn't seen him anywheres.

And the place, the queerest way in the world, had turned lonesome right that second, just by his being there.

For me, anyway, the whole feel of that moment comes back now, while I set it down here slower and more careful than I ever told it before. It was like everything else in the scene had been struck dead, while I took in what I took in. I can hear it again as I write—how hushed it got, and how the evening sounds dropped off into it. The rooks quit cawing up in the gold sky, and the friendly hour lost every bit of its voice for the minute.

But there warn't no other change in nature, unless you count that I seen things with a stranger kind of sharpness. The gold was still in the sky and the clearness was still in the air, and the man looking at me over the battlements was as plain as a picture in a frame. That's how I come to think, uncommon quick, of every person he might have been and warn't. We stood facing each other across all that distance long enough for me to ask myself hard who he was, then; and because I couldn't say, I got to wondering, and in a few seconds more the wondering was terrible strong.

Afterwards, I know, the big question about some kinds of things, or one of the big questions, is how long they lasted. Well, this thing of mine, think what you like of it, lasted while I grabbed at a dozen notions; and not a one of them, as far as I could see, made it any better that there had been somebody in the house I didn't know about—and for how long, most of all? It lasted while I got my back up a little, just barely, feeling that my place called for no such not-knowing and no such person.

It lasted while this visitor, anyway, seemed to fix me from where he stood with the very same question, the very same looking-over through the going light, that his being there had stirred up in me. There was something kind of strange and free about him, I remember, in his not wearing no hat; it was a sign he was at home there.

We was too far apart to call out to each other. But there was a moment when, if we had been closer, some kind of challenge between us, breaking up that hush, would have been the right thing to come of us staring straight at each other that way.

He was in one of the corners, the one away from the house, standing very straight, it struck me, with both hands on the ledge. So I saw him, the way I see the letters I'm making on this page. Then, after a minute exactly, like he wanted to add to the show, he moved his place, slow. He passed over to the opposite corner of the platform, and he looked at me hard the whole time. Yes, I had the sharpest kind of feeling that while he was crossing he never took his eyes off me; and I can see right now how his hand went along, as he went, from one notch of the battlements to the next.

He stopped at the other corner, but not so long, and even while he was turning away he kept me fixed. He turned away; that was all I knew.
